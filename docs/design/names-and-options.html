4 · Clicking a ghost — flights, both ways

Option 3 was chosen, so this is the interaction that has to work. Velmira has eight neighbours. Five sit close enough to draw; the other three are far away and stand in the ring as hollow ghosts — Corwen to the right, Thessa below, Doria above. All three real nodes are off screen, and you have never seen any of them.

Click any ghost. It is not deleted — it travels home. Watch what stays still: the ghost drifts gently to the middle of the screen while the whole map streams past behind it, then dissolves into the real node as that node takes over as the centre. Velmira, now the far one, is left behind as a ghost in the new ring — click that to fly back, and go round as often as you like.

or click the ghosts themselves, in either direction

Three things in there are load-bearing. The ghost survives the click — if it were torn down the moment Velmira stopped being the centre, the thing under your cursor would vanish on the second frame and you would fly blind. The flight is timed to the distance, not the flat 300ms focus uses today, which reads as a jump-cut over this far. And the rule is whichever ghost you clicked is the one that travels — not "play the outbound flight in reverse". Reversing it moves the wrong ghost: the one you clicked sits still while the one waiting at the far end flies off to meet you. Both directions are the same rule applied to whatever was clicked.

A single number cannot be right for the app, because the distances vary about tenfold: a close neighbour is ~110 units away and a far one can be 900. Fixed at 300ms, the near hop is fine and the far one is a jump-cut; fixed at 1200ms, the far one is fine and every near hop drags. What should stay roughly constant is the speed across the screen, which is what the eye actually judges:

duration = clamp(320ms, screenPixels / 0.75, 900ms), where screenPixels = worldDistance × zoom

Screen pixels rather than world units, because zoomed out the same world distance is a shorter visual move and should not take longer. That formula gives 320ms for a close neighbour (the floor), 724ms for figure 4's flight, and 900ms for a very long one (the ceiling, so the longest flights are allowed to be slightly faster than the rest — a proportionally long flight feels broken). So what figure 5 is really asking you to judge is the 0.75.

6 · Where exploration is asymmetric

One row in there is a hole in the design rather than an observation: "is the ring there when you land". Clicking a ghost flies you to a node that is usually a frontier node, and today a fetch is only triggered once the camera has settled — SETTLE_MS is 190ms after the last viewport event in main.ts, and then the request still has to go out. So the honest sequence is: fly 720ms, land, wait 190ms, fetch, and only then does the ring appear. You would sit on a bare centre for half a second or more, having just been shown a ghost that promised a neighbourhood.

The fix falls out of the asymmetry: the destination is known the instant the ghost is clicked, so start the fetch when the flight starts, not when the camera stops. The flight is 720ms of otherwise idle time — enough for most responses to arrive before the landing. Flying back needs none of this, which is exactly why the two directions are not the same piece of work.

Recommendation, and what was decided

Flight speed: 720ms for 543 units, which fixes the constant at 0.75 px/ms — duration = clamp(320ms, worldDistance × zoom / 0.75, 900ms). That replaces the flat 300ms in focus.

Decided: option 3, ghosts only. No tether on the ghost. That keeps the ring clean, and it means a ring slot no longer says anything about where the real node is: a neighbour 110 units away and one 700 units away sit at the same radius, in a direction chosen by whichever seat was free. Distance and direction are readable only by flying there. That is the trade being accepted, and it belongs in the ADR as a consequence rather than a surprise.

For the record, the analysis argued for option 1: far neighbours get rarer once the other two causes are fixed — the centre being fetched in full, and neighbours no longer dropped when the seating search runs out of room — so most of what looks hidden today will simply arrive in the ring, as in figure 2. The cost that stays is a name appearing twice on a map whose whole claim is one dot per node. Figure 4 is the mitigation: the duplicate is visibly hollow, it is the only thing on the map allowed to move, and clicking it resolves it into the original rather than leaving two copies standing.
